The lighthouse had not been lit in thirty years, and yet every autumn someone climbed the stairs to
wind the clock. Mara's grandmother had done it, and her mother after that, and now Mara carried the
brass key on a loop of string around her wrist, the way you carry something you do not entirely
believe in.

The stairs turned eleven times. She had counted them as a child and counted them still, not because
she might forget but because the counting kept the dark at a manageable distance. At the top, the
lamp room smelled of cold metal and old rain. The great lens sat under a canvas sheet like a
sleeping animal, and beyond it the sea went about its business, indifferent and enormous.

What the clock did, nobody could quite say. It did not drive the lamp; that mechanism had been sold
for scrap before Mara was born. It did not keep time for the town, which had a perfectly good clock
on the church. It simply ran, and someone wound it, and the arrangement had survived three
generations without ever being examined too closely.

She fitted the key and turned it until the spring resisted. Somewhere below her feet the mechanism
took up the tension with a sound like a held breath. Then she pulled the canvas back, because she
always did, and looked out through the lens at a horizon bent into rings of light.

There was a ship out there. There was often a ship out there. But this one was not moving, and it
was not, as far as she could tell, floating either. It sat on the water the way a word sits on a
page, deliberate and placed, and when she blinked it was still there, and when she looked away and
looked back it had come no closer and gone no further.

Mara wound the clock again, past the point where it wanted to stop, and went down to tell someone.
