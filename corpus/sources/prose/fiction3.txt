My grandmother kept her accounts in a cash book with a marbled cover, in a hand that got smaller as
the years went on, as if she were trying to fit more of her life onto each page. Tea. Coal. Shoes
mended. A pound to the chapel at Easter and another at harvest. Between 1953 and 1961 there is an
entry every month for three shillings and sixpence, marked only "S.", and nobody in the family has
ever known who S. was.

We found the book when we cleared the house, in a drawer with the good scissors and a tin of buttons
and a photograph of a man in an army greatcoat standing in front of a gate I recognised, because it
is still there, and still does not close properly. My mother took the photograph. My uncle took the
scissors, which were genuinely excellent. I took the cash book because nobody else wanted it, and
because I had been the one to find it, and because at thirty-one I had begun to be interested in the
question of how people make a life out of a small and fixed amount of money.

It is not a sad book. That surprised me. There are years in it when the coal goes up and the shoes do
not get mended, and there is one autumn where the only entries for six weeks are flour and paraffin,
and you can feel something pressing on the household from outside the edges of the page. But there is
also, every single year without exception, an entry in the first week of June for flowers, and it is
never a small amount.

I have the book on the shelf above my desk now, next to the manuals for appliances I no longer own. I
do not look at it often. But when I am trying to decide whether some expense is justified, I find I
am asking a question that the book taught me, which is not "can I afford this" but "what would this
sit next to." Tea. Coal. Shoes mended. Flowers, every June, no matter what.
