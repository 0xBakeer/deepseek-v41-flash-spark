The first thing you learn on the night shift is that the building is not empty. It creaks and settles
and runs its pumps; the lifts go up and down on schedules nobody set; somewhere on four, a printer
wakes at 3:12 every morning and produces a single blank page. Marta had worked security in the tower
for eight months and had stopped finding any of it strange, which was, she thought, either
professionalism or the beginning of something worse.

Her round took forty minutes if she did it properly, which she did, because the cameras recorded her
doing it and because the alternative was sitting in the lobby with her own thoughts. Basement, plant
room, loading bay. Then up the fire stairs to twelve, and down floor by floor, testing doors.

On the Tuesday it rained hard enough to sound like static against the glass, and on nine she found a
door propped open with a fire extinguisher. This was not unusual; the cleaners did it, the smokers
did it, once an executive had done it and denied it with real conviction. What was unusual was that
nine had been empty since March, stripped to the slab, not even carpet. She stood in the doorway with
her torch and looked at a floor of bare concrete stretching away into the dark, and at a single desk
in the middle of it, and at a lamp on the desk that was on.

She reported it. She wrote it in the log. She went back the next night and the desk was gone, and the
door was shut and locked, and the extinguisher was in its bracket where it should be, and the seal on
the bracket was intact, which it could not have been.

The supervisor listened to all of it with the patience of a man who had heard stranger things and
believed none of them, and then told her that nine was leased from the first of the month and that
the tenant had been in to measure up. Which explained the desk. Which did not explain the seal, but
Marta had learned that you can hold two facts apart in your head indefinitely if you never put them
in the same sentence.

She kept doing the round properly. She kept writing the log. And every night after that, going down
the fire stairs from twelve, she found she had started counting the floors out loud, quietly, the way
you do when you want to be sure of something you have never once been wrong about.
