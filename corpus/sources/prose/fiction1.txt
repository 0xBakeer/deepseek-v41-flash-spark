Ivo had been driving for six hours when the road stopped pretending to be a road. The tarmac gave
out at a cattle grid, and beyond it the track ran on in two ruts of pale dust with a spine of grass
between them. He got out to stretch and stood listening. There was wind in the pines and, under it,
the particular silence of a place where nobody is waiting for you.

The house was where his aunt's letter said it would be, though the letter had been written eleven
years earlier and had described a house with a roof. What remained had the dignity of a thing that
had stopped apologising. Two walls, a chimney, a doorway with no door and, improbably, a bootscraper
set into the step, as if someone might still arrive with mud on them.

He walked the boundary first, the way she had taught him, counting paces and noting the corners.
Eighty-four paces along the road, then the wall turned uphill through hazel and bramble. Somewhere in
the third field he found the well, capped with a millstone that took him twenty minutes to shift, and
underneath it water so cold it made his fingers ache, and so clear that the bucket seemed to hang in
nothing.

That evening he made a fire against the chimney wall and ate bread and cheese and an apple that had
gone soft on one side. The light went out of the sky slowly, the way it does in the north, and the
first star came up over the ridge exactly where she had said it would. He had not believed that part
of the letter either.

In the morning there was frost on the grass and a fox sitting at the edge of the trees, watching him
with the frank indifference of an animal that has never been hunted. Ivo did not move. The fox
considered him for a while, decided he was a feature of the landscape rather than an event, and went
about its business along the wall, stopping twice to listen for something under the stones.

He stayed eleven days. He mended nothing, planned nothing, drew no conclusions. He cut back the
bramble from the doorway because it caught his sleeve, and then, having started, cut it back from the
whole south wall, and then discovered under it a stone bench and a bed of nettles that had once been
something else. On the ninth day he found the initials cut into the lintel, three sets of them, the
last one his aunt's, and a date that was not the date of anything he knew about.

When he drove back down the track the ruts were wetter and the car slid twice. At the cattle grid he
stopped and looked back and could see nothing of the house at all, only the ridge and the pines and
the particular way the light came through them. He put the letter in the glove box, under the manual
and the tyre gauge, and did not throw it away.
