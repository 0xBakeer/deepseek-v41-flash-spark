There is a particular kind of failure that only happens to organisations that are trying hard. It is
not the failure of the lazy or the incompetent, which is legible and therefore fixable. It is the
failure that comes from a group of careful people each optimising the part of the system they can
see, and thereby producing a whole that nobody would have chosen.

Consider a hospital that measures the time from a patient's arrival to their first contact with a
clinician. The measure is sensible. It was chosen because a long wait before anyone assesses you is
genuinely dangerous, and because the alternative measures were worse. Within a year, the hospital's
performance against the target improves substantially, and within two years, the improvement has
stopped meaning anything, because the organisation has learned to produce a brief early contact that
satisfies the definition without changing the patient's actual path through the building.

Nobody set out to game the measure. That is the part outsiders find hard to believe. What happened
instead is that a hundred small decisions, each locally reasonable, were made slightly differently
because of what was being counted, and the aggregate of those decisions was a system that looked
better and worked the same.

The usual response is to add a second measure to catch the distortion the first one caused. This
works for a while. The trouble is that the second measure is also a target, and so the same process
begins again, and organisations that go far enough down this path end up with a wall of indicators
that nobody can hold in mind at once and that collectively describe an institution that does not
exist.

The alternative is not to stop measuring, which is a fantasy, but to be much more careful about the
relationship between a measure and the thing it stands for. A measure that is close to the thing you
care about degrades slowly under pressure. A measure that is a convenient proxy for the thing you
care about degrades quickly, because the gap between the proxy and the reality is exactly the space
in which the distortion lives. Most organisational measures are proxies, chosen because they were
available rather than because they were close, and most of the surprise that follows is not really
surprising.
